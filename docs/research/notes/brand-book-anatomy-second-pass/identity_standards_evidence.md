# Direct academic evidence on written brand standards: second pass

**Result of checking the negative finding.** The report's wording "there is almost no direct evidence" turned out to be too strong for large organizations, and for small firms it held up and became firmer. The full text of van den Bosch's 2005 dissertation was found: it has a survey, coefficients, focus groups, and audits of three organizations. But this is one Dutch dataset from 2000–2001 with self-assessment. No audit of produced materials, no experiment, and no study of agencies as users of a brand book was found. The composition of the core and periphery in Jordá-Albiñana 2009 remained unavailable: there is no legitimate open copy in the places checked.

**How to read.** The label `digest` is on everything the tool returned as a model's retelling, including search results and pages with abstracts. The label `text` is only where a PDF saved by the fetch tool was parsed into text and read directly. 22 of 50 WebSearch queries and about 90 tool calls were used; because of the call limit some of the work is not done, and it is listed in the Gaps sections. The queries behind negative findings are given where the finding is made.

## 2. Van den Bosch: 2005 dissertation and 2004–2006 papers

### Takeaway
The dissertation is open and was read. Chapters 4–7 analyze one survey of 686 employees of 20 Dutch organizations, run from November 2000 to May 2001. The currency of the rules is tied to perceived consistency almost as strongly as the example set by managers, while the detail of the rules and templates, as separate measures, are not tied to it. These are correlations with self-assessment, and there was no audit of materials.

### Cited Findings
The source for all items in this section, except where noted: [van den Bosch 2005, PDF](https://ris.utwente.nl/ws/files/6087523/thesis_A.vandenBosch.pdf), [record](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/corporate-visual-identity-management-current-practices-impact-and/), DOI 10.3990/1.9789036522656, access `text`, pages by printed numbering. Chapters 4–7 were read, chapter 9 up to the suggestions for future work (pp. 137–146), chapter 8 selectively. Chapter 5 is a reprint of the [2004](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/managing-corporate-visual-identity-use-and-effects-of-organizatio/) paper (Public Relations Review 30(2): 225–234). Chapter 6 is the accepted version of the [2006](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/03090560610670034/full/html) paper in the European Journal of Marketing. Chapter 7 is a version of the 2006 paper in the Journal of Business Communication, DOI [10.1177/0021943605285476](https://doi.org/10.1177/0021943605285476). The journal versions were not checked separately. Chapter 3, the 2005 paper (Corporate Communications 10(2): 108–116), is theoretical according to the author; the chapter was not read in full.

**Sample** [EMPIRICAL: cross-sectional survey, Netherlands]
- 80 organizations were approached and 20 took part, each with more than 400 employees and no identity change in the last two years: 9 commercial (N=274), 11 nonprofit (N=412); 4 manufacturing (N=144), 16 service (N=542) (pp. 102–103).
- Contact persons from communication departments themselves selected about 70 employees each whose work involves the rules. Response 48%, from 13% to 78%. 686 questionnaires (pp. 52, 82).
- 17% are directly involved in the identity (N=114), 83% indirectly (N=572); 29% managers, 71% rank-and-file (pp. 52, 72).
- An organization's response rate correlates with its consistency rating, r = .46, p < .05 (p. 72).

**Dependent variable** — perceived consistency, 8 statements, five-point scale, α = .80, mean 3.51, SD = .67, from 2.38 to 3.92 across organizations (pp. 52, 59, 83). Scale content: the organization is easy to recognize by its identity; the identity suits it; the identity looks sloppy (reversed); everyone contributes to a good image; everyone follows the rules; it matters to me to apply the identity; the materials lack uniformity (reversed); everyone applies it as they see fit (reversed) (table 6.1, p. 84). The questionnaire was in Dutch, and no back-translation was done (p. 85).

**Ten measures** (structural and cultural; answer yes, no, don't know) and their link to consistency. Stepwise regression, "no" and "don't know" combined, adj. R² = .26, F = 37.758 (tables 5.1–5.4, pp. 71–74; subgroups: enter method, table 7.5, p. 111):

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Technical aids, such as templates | 75 (10/15) | 80 | not entered | .07 / .05 / .06 / .06 |
| Access to the rules for everyone | 54 (22/25) | 68 | .08* | .23* / .03 / .12 / .05 |
| Up-to-date rules | 52 (23/26) | 67 | .18** | .00 / .21** / .13 / .16* |
| Identity manager | 47 (32/22) | 48 | .16** | .16 / .13* / .15* / .12* |
| Preferred suppliers | 46 (17/36) | 52 | .14** | .15 / .12* / .18* / .10* |
| Detailed rules | 39 (35/26) | 37 | not entered | .10 / .01 / .00 / .03 |
| Helpdesk | 32 (50/17) | 45 | not entered | .01 / .03 / −.05 / .07 |

\* p < .05, \** p < .001. In the subgroups R² = .29 / .28 / .25 / .28, N = 124 / 503 / 242 / 385; there are only four manufacturing organizations.

- If the number of measures applied is added, it becomes the strongest predictor: β = .37; managers' example .14; up-to-date rules .10; adj. R² = .26, F = 74.013 (table 5.5, p. 75).
- Having a measure strongly raises the rating of its importance: Mann-Whitney, p < .001 (p. 73). Employees tend to value what they already have.
- Answers within an organization diverge: at one large university 11 people said the rules were up to date, 10 said they were not, and 10 did not know (p. 72).
- Managers more often believe they set an example: 39% versus 22%, χ² = 19.795 (p. 75).

**Three management scales** (tables 6.1, 6.2, pp. 84–88; `r` with consistency):
- Knowledge of the identity strategy, 3 items, α = .70, r = .55: I know the meaning of the logo; the identity helps recognition; it creates a good image.
- Tools and support, 5 items, α = .85, r = .60: enough information; the rules are up to date; there are means for easy application; the information is easy to get; questions get good answers.
- Socialization, 5 items, α = .73, r = .58: managers set an example; newcomers are always introduced to the topic; applying it is taken for granted; people are criticized for not applying it; people are praised for applying it.
- Among themselves: tools and socialization .58, tools and knowledge .44, knowledge and socialization .33.

**Model.** A structural model in AMOS: R² of consistency = .50; χ² = 49.385, df = 14, p < .001; GFI .985; TLI .960; RMSEA .061. Two paths, from tools to knowledge and to socialization, were added by modification indices and were not hypothesized in advance. For the mediators R² is from .13 to .37. Of the organization's general characteristics, only openness and dynamism is directly linked (pp. 89–92). The coefficients of figure 6.1 come without arrows in the text layer, so we do not attach them to specific paths.
- The same three scales in the subgroup regression (table 7.3, p. 109; R² .62 / .53 / .59 / .49): socialization β .30–.33, knowledge .27–.29, tools .22–.24, all significant.
- Commercial organizations rate consistency higher: 3.66 versus 3.41, t = 4.69, p < .001; manufacturing and services do not differ, 3.56 and 3.49 (table 7.2, p. 108). 67% in commercial and 41% in nonprofit organizations say the rules are up to date (table 7.4, p. 110).

**Why templates are unrelated in one chapter and related in another.** The author explains this by the wide availability of templates and by the fact that the composite scale in chapter 6 also includes information, currency, and support (p. 138).

**Content and carrier, survey of the involved** (N=114, self-report, data from 2000–2001; answers within organizations diverge; pp. 53–59):
- Elements defined in the identity: colors 95%, logo 86%, standard fonts 81%, grids 65%, additional graphic elements 64%, custom-made fonts 52%, slogan 41%, symbols 28%.
- Areas of application with rules: print 97%, office documents 96%, advertising 95%, stationery and forms 94%, internet and intranet 83%, signage 80%, flags 80%, souvenirs 75%, presentations 72%, vehicles 67%, packaging 53%, workwear 43%.
- Scope of the identity: visual 97%, language and tone 24%, regulations 14%, rules of behavior 10%, dress code 7%.
- Available reference material on the intent: visual language 55%, logo 51%, desired identity 34%.
- Where the indirectly involved (N=572) get information: paper manual 58%, digital on the network 23%. 24% wrote in their own: an A4 sheet once and for all, colleagues, the communications department, suppliers, did something myself, unavailable.

**Qualitative data on forgetting and non-compliance** [EMPIRICAL; focus groups and audits, Netherlands]
- Three focus groups, July 2001, organizations with high, medium, and low consistency (3.84, 3.30, 3.09), 9, 7, and 7 participants (pp. 59–60). Complaints: information is outdated and scattered, not everyone received the handbook and it quickly became outdated, the report template was called junk, the rules are vague, almost nobody knew about the new presentation template (p. 61).
- The most consistent organization and the least consistent one had the same problems with rules and templates (p. 61). Differences were found elsewhere: planned rollout and a coordinator at the first, a meager budget and no coordinator at the last, pride and identification with the organization (pp. 61–63). The author explicitly warns that the study is exploratory (p. 65).
- The wish to know "why" is uneven: some participants want to understand the philosophy, others ask only for instructions (p. 62).
- An identity management audit in three organizations: an energy company, a pension fund, a pharmaceutical company; document analysis, an introductory interview, a self-assessment session, 13, 15, and 9 interviews (table 8.3, p. 130). In the fund, the government relations department considered itself compliant, went its own way, and worked next to a CEO indifferent to the identity; another self-confident department could not tell a correct letterhead layout from an incorrect one. In the pharmaceutical company, IT did not install the templates because it was not written into their procedures, and the work stopped at the intent and did not reach rules for marketing (pp. 131–133). In the first company, internal and external suppliers were interviewed, and the main recommendations concerned procurement and service level agreements (p. 131). These are case descriptions of a consulting instrument, with no control group.

**The author's position on auditing materials.** The author herself admits that the dependent variable is subjective and that an objective measure might have given different findings (p. 77). Reasons for declining: an audit needs up-to-date and unambiguous rules, a large collection of materials, and a separate validated scale (p. 144). The author's position: rules are "necessary, but not sufficient" (van den Bosch 2005, p. 140).

### Inferences
- [RESEARCHER INFERENCE] Causality is not shown. All variables come from one person at one moment, with common method variance and selection: contact persons chose the respondents, and organizations with a better rating responded more often. A multilevel model is not mentioned in the chapters read, although respondents are nested in 20 organizations. The author's words "influences" and "impact" are not supported by the data.
- [RESEARCHER INFERENCE] The consistency scale partly overlaps with the predictors: compliance with the rules and their personal importance are in the dependent variable, and "applying it is taken for granted" is in socialization. So R² = .50 is probably inflated.
- [RESEARCHER INFERENCE] Since "no" and "don't know" are combined, a predictor means "the employee knows about the measure", not "the measure exists". This matters for a brand book: the result says that the rules reached people, not only that they were written.
- [RESEARCHER INFERENCE] These data weakly support the contrast "people are stronger than tools". The pairwise correlations are almost equal (.60 for tools and .58 for socialization), and in the regression the difference is small. Besides, socialization includes criticism for non-compliance, that is, an element of control. This removes part of the dispute between Henkel and Demand Metric in section 5 of the report, but does not settle it.
- [RESEARCHER INFERENCE] The detail of the rules is not related to perceived consistency, but this does not prove that a short document is better. The measure was rated as yes or no, and no one measured or changed length.
- [RESEARCHER INFERENCE] There is no direct transfer to small companies: a minimum of 400 employees, more than half the organizations are nonprofit, the data are 25 years old, and the main carrier is paper.

### Gaps
- The author suggests requesting the questionnaire from her (p. 52); the full text of the questions about the measures and the scales in the Dutch original is not visible, and the items above are retold from the author's English table.
- Attaching the coefficients of figure 6.1 to paths is not established: the figure from the PDF or the journal version is needed.
- Pointers from the dissertation itself, not checked: van Gemert 2003 on infection prevention protocols and Elling 1991 on safety rules (p. 143), that is, compliance with written rules in other fields; Van den Bosch 1999, 2000; Roos 2000.

## 3. Literature on corporate visual identity that the first pass did not reach

### Takeaway
The works of Melewar and Saunders exist, but they study standardization and control on an international scale, not the content and observance of a document. The scale of Simões and co-authors is confirmed at the level of descriptions, with no full text. A new and direct observation from a Spanish sample was found: among the 100 companies with the best reputation, 21 have a brand center, and 18 of them have it closed.

Headquarters control and standardization go together, the main internal force behind a global standard is the CEO, and local managers, contrary to expectations, support standardization about as much as headquarters. The sample size is not visible — [Middlesex](https://repository.mdx.ac.uk/item/84798) (the page itself returned 405, the content is taken from search results).

### Inferences
- [RESEARCHER INFERENCE] The Melewar line studies where the standard is decided and what predicts it, and it surveys managers. The document itself and its application are not measured in the available descriptions. So this line does not overturn the negative finding about documents.
- [RESEARCHER INFERENCE] The "after the manual" hypothesis of Gregersen and Johansen comes from people who sell dynamic systems and contains no data on outcome. This is an industry opinion, not proof. The Dutch data from 2001 and this opinion do not directly contradict each other: one is about consistency inside an organization, the other about digital media 14 years later.
- [RESEARCHER INFERENCE] The Spanish sample data strengthen the report's caveat about bias: at leading companies the working rules more often live behind a contractual wall, and the open web shows logos for the press.

## 4. Do those outside the core team follow the standards

### Takeaway
No study was found where an independent coder checks produced materials against a brand book. The closest substitute by design is in the economics of franchising: outside inspections show that franchises hold the chain's standard worse while the result is not visible to the buyer. This is a transfer from hygiene and labor law to brand.

### Cited Findings
- [TRANSFER; `text`, descriptive section] Jin, Leslie 2009, American Economic Journal: Microeconomics 1(1): 237–267: inspections of Los Angeles restaurants, July 1995 — December 1998; 24,304 restaurants, 127,111 inspections, 2,632 chain restaurants (about 11%), of which about 63% are owned by the chain. Before score cards were introduced, company-owned outlets are about one point better than franchised ones; afterward the difference becomes negligible. For Burger King the gap falls from 4.89 to 0.1 points. The authors interpret this as free-riding on the chain's reputation — [PDF UMD](https://econ.umd.edu/sites/www.econ.umd.edu/files/pubs/ReputationAEJFinalVersion.pdf). What transfers: the check is external, not self-report, and participants do not bear the full cost of damage to the shared brand.
