# Why brand books get used or forgotten, and how reliable the data is

Tags: [EMPIRICAL], [TRANSFER], [PRIMARY-SOURCE FACT], [EXPERT OPINION], [VENDOR], [RESEARCHER INFERENCE].

Source access codes:
- **FT** (full text): I read the extracted text myself, in full or almost in full.
- **FT-part.** (full text, in part): the text was available, and I read the method, the results, and a keyword search.
- **PAGE**: the page was read through a tool that returns a paraphrase, not verbatim text. Check the numbers before publishing.
- **ABS** (abstract): only the abstract or a repository record.
- **SNIPPET**: only search results or other authors' descriptions.
- **BIB** (bibliographic): only the existence of the work is confirmed, and I did not see the content.

## 1. Is there direct empirical evidence on how employees, agencies, and partners use brand guidelines?

### Takeaway
[RESEARCHER INFERENCE] There is almost no direct data. The closest fit is one Dutch dataset from the early 2000s (van den Bosch and co-authors, 20 organizations, employee perceptions) and vendor surveys. I found no observational study, log analysis, or experiment on how people consult a brand book, and no study of agencies or partners as users.

### Cited Findings
- [EMPIRICAL] van den Bosch, de Jong, Elving (2004), Public Relations Review 30(2). A survey of employees of 20 large Dutch organizations, comparing structural and cultural measures on three dimensions: application, perceived importance, effectiveness. The match between these dimensions is weak. Templates and access to current guidelines are common and considered important, but the key factor turned out to be leaders' personal example, a measure that got little attention. The design is cross-sectional, correlational. The number of respondents is not stated on the page. Access: ABS. — [Twente](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/managing-corporate-visual-identity-use-and-effects-of-organizatio/), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pubrev.2003.12.002)
- [EMPIRICAL] van den Bosch, Elving, de Jong (2006), European Journal of Marketing 40(7/8): 870–885. A survey of employees of 20 organizations, SEM in AMOS. Three factors showed a strong influence on consistency: socialization around the visual identity, knowledge of its strategy, tools and support. Null result: apart from openness and dynamism, no direct link between an organization's characteristics and consistency was found. An important limit noted by the authors: consistency was measured as employees' perception, not by a visual audit or by assessment from outside audiences. Causality is not shown. Access: ABS. — [Emerald](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/03090560610670034/full/html)
- [EMPIRICAL] van den Bosch, de Jong, Elving (2006), Journal of Business Communication 43(2): 138–157. Employee questionnaires from 20 organizations, comparing manufacturing and services, for-profit and nonprofit organizations. The main difference runs between for-profit and nonprofit: for-profits have higher consistency, and the authors link this to greater effort in managing identity. Access: ABS. — [Hanze](https://research.hanze.nl/nl/publications/managing-corporate-visual-identity-exploring-the-differences-betw/)
- [EMPIRICAL] Baker, Balmer (1997), European Journal of Marketing 31(5/6): 366–382. A case of a British university: a visual identity audit helps reveal organizational weaknesses, and a weak identity can be a symptom of general trouble. I looked in this work for data on employees' non-compliance with guidelines and did not confirm any. Access: SNIPPET. — [DOI](https://www.doi.org/10.1108/EB060637)
- [EMPIRICAL] An article in Journal of Brand Management (2025) on aligning employees with brand promises. Participatory action research in a Nordic elderly care organization. Factors: clarity of the promises, time to deliver them, employee participation in creating the promises. This is about brand promises, not the brand book. Access: SNIPPET. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41262-025-00401-3)
- [VENDOR] The only numbers I found on the guidelines themselves: Demand Metric commissioned by Lucidpress, 2016, n=234. 49% rated the effectiveness of their guidelines as good or very good, 40% rated ease of finding them the same, 61% ease of following them, 64% how current they are. One person per organization answered, mostly from marketing. This is self-assessment, not user behavior. Access: FT. — [PDF](https://d2slcw3kip6qmk.cloudfront.net/marketing/press/webinar/The-Impact-of-Brand-Consistency-Report-New_Cover.pdf)
- [VENDOR] The same source has only data on rights for partners: resellers or partners can create branded materials for 8–12% of respondents, franchisees for 4%. An agency as the main brand owner is named in 1% of cases. The survey did not ask how they use the guidelines. I reconstructed the distribution of the series from the logic of the text, because the labels on the charts in the PDF are not marked up. Access: FT. — [PDF](https://d2slcw3kip6qmk.cloudfront.net/marketing/press/webinar/The-Impact-of-Brand-Consistency-Report-New_Cover.pdf)
- [VENDOR] empower with Kantar, a 2024 survey in a 2025 report: 76% of external documents comply with guidelines, 58% find compliance annoying and slow, 52% admit using outdated materials. The sample size is not stated on the page. The page describes the share of non-compliant documents in different ways. The company sells tools for templates in Office. Access: PAGE. — [empower](https://www.empowersuite.com/en/knowledge/e-books/pp-corporate-design-report-25-02)

### Inferences
- [RESEARCHER INFERENCE] The three van den Bosch articles, judging by the descriptions, rest on one set of 20 organizations. They are not three independent checks. I compared the samples of the three articles only through abstracts.
- [RESEARCHER INFERENCE] The most valuable detail for the report: tools and access to guidelines matter, but these works do not answer whether a good document is needed, because the document did not vary there. What was measured was perceived consistency, not use.
- [RESEARCHER INFERENCE] Any claim "a brand book is used if it is X" currently rests on self-assessment or on transfer from adjacent fields. This must be stated plainly.

### Gaps
- Not found: logs of guideline portals, observation of work with a brand book, comparison of formats under controlled conditions, studies of agencies and partners. The absence of results is partly due to the search limit, so it is weak evidence of absence.
- I could not find van den Bosch's dissertation (2005): the search broke off. It could have given the sample and the instruments.
- A search paraphrase turned up a study with 18 interviews and 313 respondents at state-owned enterprises on the superiority of informal control over formal. I did not establish the authors or the primary source, and it cannot be used. The same applies to a work on employee behavior on social media and policies: [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/309152447_Brand_consistent_behavior_of_employees_on_social_media_the_role_of_social_media_governance_and_policies), the page is closed.

## 2. Internal branding: what makes employees understand the brand and act on it, and how strong are the effects?

### Takeaway
[RESEARCHER INFERENCE] The literature is large, with at least three meta-analyses. The direction is stable: communication and leadership are linked to brand commitment and behavior more strongly than HR measures are. I could not confirm the magnitude, because I saw no pooled coefficient. The base is mostly cross-sectional and self-reported, from the services sector. The only causal element I found is the field experiment by Morhart and co-authors, and it concerns training leaders, not documents. None of these works measured brand book use.

### Cited Findings
- [EMPIRICAL] Afshardoost, Eshaghi, Bowden (2023), Journal of Strategic Marketing 31(5): 983–1011. Meta-analysis, 38 studies. Internal brand management significantly predicts commitment and brand citizenship behaviour. For commitment, the main predictor is brand communication, then leadership. For behavior, among the management measures leadership is strongest, and brand understanding has the greatest influence on behavior. HR measures give a moderate effect. Coefficients are not given on the page. Access: ABS. — [Macquarie](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/internal-brand-management-brand-understanding-employee-brand-comm/), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/0965254X.2021.2016896)
- [EMPIRICAL] Prashar, Maity (2024), European Journal of Marketing 58(5): 1241–1274. Meta-analysis, 65 studies from 62 papers, 226 effect sizes. The link with commitment is moderated by education, age, gender, tenure, employment status, and customer orientation. I did not see coefficients: the PDF is closed. Access: SNIPPET. — [EconBiz](https://www.econbiz.de/Record/building-employee-commitment-through-internal-branding-a-meta-analytic-study-prashar-atul/10015047511), [manuscript](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/489418)
- [EMPIRICAL] Chhibber, Chahal, Kaurav (2025), Management Review Quarterly 75(2): 1259–1310. Meta-analysis, 31 studies from 2009–2023 in the services sector. Brand commitment partly mediates the link between internal branding and behavior. Country type and sector have a significant influence. The significance of direct and indirect links differs between studies. Access: ABS. — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/manrev/v75y2025i2d10.1007_s11301-024-00408-1.html)
- [EMPIRICAL] Morhart, Herzog, Tomczak (2009), Journal of Marketing 73(5): 122–142. A survey of 269 customer-facing employees: transactional brand leadership works through compliance, transformational through internalization. A field experiment showed that transformational brand leadership can be taught. This is the strongest causal evidence in the section, but I did not see the size and conditions of the experiment. Access: SNIPPET. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkg.73.5.122), [EconBiz](https://econbiz.de/Record/brand-specific-leadership-turning-employees-into-brand-champions-morhart-felicitas/10003875427)
- [EMPIRICAL] Henkel, Tomczak, Heitmann, Herrmann (2007), Journal of Product & Brand Management 16(4/5): 310–320. PLS on 167 top managers and focus groups. Informal management and empowerment influence brand-consistent behavior much more strongly than formal instruments. Managers answered, not employees, and this is a correlation. Access: ABS. — [Emerald](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/10610420710779609/full/html)
- [EMPIRICAL] Punjaisri, Wilson (2007), Journal of Brand Management 15(1): 57–70. A mixed-methods case, a survey of 699 respondents (per related publications, employees of five hotels in Thailand). Internal branding is positively linked to delivering the brand promise directly and through identification, commitment, and loyalty. The abstract does not separate the contribution of training and communication. Correlation. Access: ABS. — [Strathclyde](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/9234/)
- [EMPIRICAL] Punjaisri, Wilson (2011), European Journal of Marketing 45(9/10): 1521–1537. 30 interviews and a survey of 680 employees of five Thai hotels. Moderators: age, education, tenure. The effect is higher with satisfaction with the work environment, and the authors consider internal branding unpromising where the environment does not support employees and brand values. The authors also note that a significant part of the literature is conceptual and based on small studies with managers and consultants. Access: SNIPPET. — [Strathclyde](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/28008/), [Emerald](https://www.emeraldinsight.com/doi/abs/10.1108/03090561111151871)
- [EMPIRICAL] Müller (2017), Organization Studies 38(7): 895–915. A qualitative case of a consumer goods company. Internal branding is described as a new form of normative control that draws in an outside audience and blurs the line between work and personal life. This is a critical line against the positive picture. It has no direct bearing on guidelines. Access: SNIPPET. — [Lund](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/publication/2fd7edad-bdd9-45ec-9a2c-94b3a4a4649d)
- [PRIMARY-SOURCE FACT] Burmann, Zeplin (2005), Journal of Brand Management 12(4): 279–300. A model with three levers (HR, communication, leadership) and four context factors (culture, structure, employee knowledge, resources). The basis is interviews with brand managers and experts, not a test of the model. Access: SNIPPET. — [DOI](https://www.doi.org/10.1057/palgrave.bm.2540223)
- [PRIMARY-SOURCE FACT] Existence confirmed, samples and results not seen: King, Grace (2010), European Journal of Marketing 44(7/8): 938–971, the abstract calls it the first empirically tested model of employee-based brand equity ([DOI](https://www.doi.org/10.1108/03090561011047472)); King, Grace (2008), Journal of Brand Management 15(5): 358–372 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1057/palgrave.bm.2550136)); Burmann, Zeplin, Riley (2009), Journal of Brand Management 16(4): 264–284 ([EconBiz](https://econbiz.de/Record/key-determinants-of-internal-brand-management-success-an-exploratory-empirical-analysis-burmann-christoph/10003811727)); Vallaster, de Chernatony (2006), European Journal of Marketing 40(7/8): 761–784 ([Birmingham](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/internal-brand-building-and-structuration-the-role-of-leadership)); Miles, Mangold (2004), Journal of Relationship Marketing 3(2/3): 65–87. Access: BIB.

### Inferences
- [RESEARCHER INFERENCE] Independent lines converge: the meta-analyses, Henkel 2007, and van den Bosch 2004 indicate that channels between people are linked to the outcome more strongly than formal instruments are. But no one directly compared a good brand book with a bad one at equal leadership. The conclusion "the document does not matter" does not follow from these data.
- [RESEARCHER INFERENCE] A meta-analysis of cross-sectional self-reports does not remove common method variance. When one person rates both internal communication and their own commitment, the link is inflated. This is a general caution; I did not check how these three meta-analyses handle it.
- [RESEARCHER INFERENCE] The outcome in this literature is customer service and citizenship behavior toward the brand. A brand book needs a different outcome: correct application of style and tone. The transfer is partial. The Thai hotels give two samples from one research program, not an independent replication.

### Gaps
- Pooled coefficients: all three meta-analyses were unavailable in full text. The report must not call the effects strong or weak without checking them.
- The sample of 371 for King and Grace (2010) is not confirmed: I remembered the figure and looked for it, but no source showed it. It cannot be used.
- Parameters of the field experiment by Morhart and co-authors. Data on the separate contribution of training and communication in Punjaisri and Wilson.

## 3. Consistency and flexibility: what is the value of consistency, when does it hurt, and does strictness cause rule evasion?

### Takeaway
[RESEARCHER INFERENCE] I found no data linking compliance with visual rules to a business result. The harm of rigid consistency is shown qualitatively and at the level of positioning, not guidelines. There is no direct test of the hypothesis "strictness breeds evasion", and the only direct numbers (vendor) show the opposite relationship. The form of strictness probably matters more than its degree, but this is transfer.

### Cited Findings
- [EMPIRICAL] Beverland, Wilner, Micheli (2015), Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science 43(5): 589–609. Grounded theory: 13 interviews with design experts in Australia and New Zealand, then 44 interviews with 24 designers and 20 brand managers in 20 firms, then a check with six designers and two seminars. Three types of tension: brand position against the market, against efficiency, and against the future. Brand managers understood that earlier attempts to solve problems through consistency had not worked. Excessive reliance on existing frames got in the way of relevant innovation. The authors explicitly note that concern for consistency is not wrong in itself. Access: FT-part. — [preprint](https://purehost.bath.ac.uk/ws/files/120696551/JAMSdesignfinal.pdf), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11747-015-0443-8)
- [PRIMARY-SOURCE FACT] In the main text of the Beverland et al. preprint, a search for the words guideline, manual, rulebook, style guide, and policing gave no matches. The work is about positioning and innovation, not brand books. Access: FT-part. — [preprint](https://purehost.bath.ac.uk/ws/files/120696551/JAMSdesignfinal.pdf)
- [VENDOR] Demand Metric 2016, the share of those who rarely or never release non-compliant materials: with formal guidelines without enforcement 24%, with informal ones 32%, with selective enforcement 38%, with consistent enforcement 58%. This relationship does not support the idea "strictness leads to being ignored". A formal document without enforcement looks worse than informal norms, but by my estimate there are about 23 respondents in that cell, and significance tests are not given. At the same time the authors note that even with consistent enforcement 42% see violations. Correlation, self-report. Access: FT. — [PDF](https://d2slcw3kip6qmk.cloudfront.net/marketing/press/webinar/The-Impact-of-Brand-Consistency-Report-New_Cover.pdf)
- [VENDOR] The same source: 40% of organizations with a consistent brand fulfill requests for materials in under a week, against 24% with an inconsistent one. The authors conclude that enforcement does not slow things down. This is a correlation, and organization size and resources were not controlled. Access: FT. — [PDF](https://d2slcw3kip6qmk.cloudfront.net/marketing/press/webinar/The-Impact-of-Brand-Consistency-Report-New_Cover.pdf)
- [EMPIRICAL] Contradiction: Henkel and co-authors (2007) show that formal instruments are weaker than informal ones, while the vendor survey calls enforcement a key factor. The outcomes differ (employee behavior and compliance of materials), and both are self-reported. — [Henkel](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/10610420710779609/full/html)
- [TRANSFER] Adler, Borys (1996), Administrative Science Quarterly 41: 61–89. Attitude toward formalization depends on whether it helps master the task or serves coercion. What transfers is the distinction between types of rules. Uncertainty: the work is conceptual, and its main example is the NUMMI plant. Access: SNIPPET, the PDF turned out to be a scan with no text layer. — [PDF](https://faculty.marshall.usc.edu/Paul-Adler/research/ASQ%20copy-1.pdf)
- [TRANSFER] Lamine, Cheng (2022), arXiv preprint: interviews with 9 design system leads. Six named the choice between customizability and standardization as a serious problem, five an unfavorable culture. One reported that people felt constrained by the system. What transfers is the tension between the center and the teams. Uncertainty: small sample, open systems, a different audience. Access: PAGE. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2205.10713v1)
- [EXPERT OPINION] Spotify: notes on Shaun Bent's talk (2019) describe the evasion of the system as a reaction to a bottleneck, not to the strictness of the rules. A single team became the bottleneck, and teams began to build their own components. The notes were written by a third party. Access: PAGE. — [Always Twisted](https://www.alwaystwisted.com/articles/design-systems-london-2019-shaun-bent)

### Inferences
- [RESEARCHER INFERENCE] For the report it is useful to separate three things: positioning consistency, execution consistency, and enforcement of compliance. The literature on the first must not confirm conclusions about the second and third.
- [RESEARCHER INFERENCE] Beverland and co-authors have a built-in bias. The firms were chosen because they themselves attribute renewal to design, half the informants are designers, and there is no control group. This is theory generation, not proof of harm from consistency.
- [RESEARCHER INFERENCE] A hypothesis to test: evasion is caused not by the strictness of the rule but by the cost of compliance at the moment of work (waiting for a designer, not finding the file, no suitable variant). There is little data in favor: 48% of Demand Metric respondents wait a week or longer for materials, plus Spotify's experience. This is not enough for a claim.

### Gaps
- Erdem, Swait (1998), Journal of Consumer Psychology 7(2) ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327663jcp0702_02)) and Park, Jaworski, MacInnis (1986), Journal of Marketing 50(4) ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/002224298605000401)): the DOIs lead to journals, the pages are closed, the content was not read. Keller, The Brand Report Card, HBR 2000 ([HBR](https://hbr.org/2000/01/the-brand-report-card)): only the introduction is visible. I did not confirm what these works say about consistency. Use only after checking.
- I could not find consumer experiments on variability of visual identity: the search limit is exhausted.
- No direct study of reactance to brand rules or of evading them was found.

## 4. Do the vendor numbers hold up?

### Takeaway
[RESEARCHER INFERENCE] No, not as evidence of an effect. The chain is traced: 23% (2016) and 33% (2019) are average marketer estimates in answer to a hypothetical question, not measured revenue growth, and the surveys were paid for or run by a seller of brand management software. The formula "consistency raises revenue by 33%" is wrong in substance. The 95%/25% figure is real but regularly distorted: the 95% includes informal guidelines.

## 5. Adjacent data: why documentation is read or ignored, and how well it transfers

### Takeaway
[RESEARCHER INFERENCE] The whole section is transfer. The most useful result runs against the popular version: in the documentation data the main problems concern content and applicability, not presentation, and an outdated document is not necessarily useless. Minimalism has real experiments, but old and small ones. Design system surveys confirm that adoption remains a problem, but they mostly survey the creators.

### Cited Findings
- [TRANSFER] Uddin, Robillard (2015), IEEE Software 32(4). Two surveys at IBM: 69 responses of 698 (9.9%), then 254 of 1,064 (23.8%). Six content problems (incompleteness, ambiguity, unexplained examples, obsolescence, inconsistency, errors) and four presentation problems (bloat, scatter, excess structure, mixing). Respondents ranked five content problems above any presentation problem. Ambiguity was often encountered by 26.5% of respondents, and 51.9% chose it as the top priority. There is no significant difference between novices and experienced users (null result). What transfers: content matters more than packaging. Uncertainty: developers can switch API, and an employee cannot switch brand; a different task and audience. Access: FT-part. — [PDF](https://www.cs.mcgill.ca/~martin/papers/ieeesw2015.pdf)
- [TRANSFER] Lethbridge, Singer, Forward (2003), IEEE Software, November/December. Three studies: interviews at 13 sites, fieldwork in a telecom company, a 50-question survey (48 respondents, 25–45 answered individual questions). 68% agree that documentation always lags, but 81% agree that it is useful even when outdated. The link between perceived accuracy and frequency of use ranges from 0.67 for test documentation to 0.03 for specifications (null result). Documentation gets updated when it is tied to the change process. Simple and powerful formats are used, complex and slow ones are ignored. The authors argue with the idea of imposing discipline: engineers assess value. What transfers: abstract principles survive details, tie-in to the workflow. Uncertainty: small sample, code has a verifiable truth and a brand does not. Access: FT. — [PDF](https://courses.cs.duke.edu//cps196.1/fall11/classwork/Lethbridge-Singer-Forward-2003.pdf)
- [TRANSFER] The same source, the gap between words and deeds: 6% claimed significant time reading documentation, while in observation documentation was opened in 3% of events (12 of 357). The authors consider that real use may be lower than claimed. What transfers is the methodological conclusion: surveys about brand book use probably overstate it. Access: FT. — [PDF](https://courses.cs.duke.edu//cps196.1/fall11/classwork/Lethbridge-Singer-Forward-2003.pdf)
- [TRANSFER] Novick, Ward (2006), ACM SIGDOC, pp. 11–18. In-depth interviews with 25 users. Few have paper manuals for their main programs, and no one complained about it. Online help is opened more often, but people are just as likely to ask a colleague or experiment. Barriers: navigation, choosing search words, a disappointing level of explanation. What transfers: a colleague competes with the document, search matters more than form. Uncertainty: 25 people, consumer software and help, not rules. Access: ABS, full text closed. — [UTEP](https://scholarworks.utep.edu/cs_papers/15)
- [TRANSFER] Novick, Ward (2006), What users say they want in documentation, SIGDOC. The same sample: they want easy navigation, the right level of explanation, completeness and correctness, online or on paper. Access: SNIPPET, the full reference has no URL.
- [TRANSFER] Carroll, Smith-Kerker, Ford, Mazur-Rimetz (1987), Human–Computer Interaction 3(2): 123–153. Experiment 1: 19 people, IBM, an office simulation, a minimal manual against a commercial one, 40% less learning time (t(17)=3.06, p<0.01). Experiment 2: 32 people, an advantage in completed subtasks. In a test of understanding the system's concepts there is no significant difference (null result). The authors themselves warn that office workers learning a word processor may not generalize. What transfers: task first, shorter, with error recovery. Uncertainty: learning over a day, not consulting rules at work. Access: FT-part. — [PDF](https://davis68.github.io/2016-12-12-ttt-uiuc/files/papers/carroll-minimal-manual-1987.pdf)
- [TRANSFER] Lazonder, van der Meij (1993), International Journal of Man-Machine Studies 39(5): 729–752. 64 students randomly assigned, replicated the result: the minimal manual is faster and better. Experience affects the result but does not interact with the type of manual. A criticism I saw in a snippet: the minimal manual was built on the control one and could simply be a second, improved version. Access: ABS; criticism SNIPPET. — [Twente](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/f811580e-953a-4312-a1ee-9916b3f98e05), [criticism](https://ris.utwente.nl/ws/files/6853553/Meij93assessment.pdf)
- [EXPERT OPINION] A page on technical communication myths disputes "no one reads": people read selectively and by task, programmers use documentation as external memory (Brandt et al., 2009, not read), the long tail of rare topics is valuable. Warning: data on web content may not apply to technical documentation. Access: PAGE. — [TC Myths](https://docsbydesign.github.io/tcmyths/myths/no-one-reads-technical-docs)
- [TRANSFER] Lamine, Cheng (2022), preprint: 4,714 GitHub issues from 41 open design systems and 9 interviews (August–October 2019). Recommended practices: a dedicated team, a bottom-up approach, proactive communication, quality documentation. Issues about documentation are about 10% of those analyzed, fewer than the authors expected. A peer-reviewed venue is not stated on the page. Access: PAGE. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2205.10713v1)
- [TRANSFER] The SAP case (ACM, 2025, Improving Design System Adoption with Inner Source): 76 interviews, barriers are knowledge gaps, slow development of the system, lack of flexibility, inefficiency. Access: SNIPPET. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706599.3706705)
- [TRANSFER] Design Systems: A Community Case Study (ACM, 2020): a snippet mentions a survey with n=1,513 and interviews at the Clarity 2019 conference; in four interviews, measuring the effect is named as a pain. I could not check the sample size. Access: SNIPPET. — [ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3334480.3375204)
- [VENDOR] Sparkbox 2021 (an agency that sells design system services): 376 responses, 217 in-house, 159 from agencies, self-selected. 44% of in-house respondents name adoption as the main challenge. By self-assessed success: 9% among the very successful, 83% among the unsuccessful, but there are about six unsuccessful respondents. 48–50% of the successful track metrics against 16–23% of the average. There is no link with tech debt (null result). Success itself is self-assessed, and the survey speaks of associations. Access: PAGE. — [Sparkbox](https://designsystemssurvey.seesparkbox.com/2021)
- [VENDOR] zeroheight 2025 (sells a tool for documenting design systems): by snippets about 300 respondents, only 2% pure consumers of systems, dedicated teams grew from 72% to 79%, 41% lack people, communication is named as the next barrier. The announcement of the 2026 report names documentation the main pain and the main opportunity, and adoption an existential challenge. There is a direct conflict of interest here. Access: SNIPPET, PDF larger than the limit. — [PDF 2025](https://othr.zeroheight.com/hubfs/zeroheight%20-%20Design%20System%20Report%202025%20-%20release%20version.pdf), [webinar 2026](https://webinars.zeroheight.com/register/zeroheight-design-systems-report-2026-the-findings)

### Inferences
- [RESEARCHER INFERENCE] How far the transfer goes. Mechanisms transfer best: people look for an answer for a task, ask colleagues, prefer what is short and applicable. Numbers transfer worse. A design system is built into a tool, its use is counted in imports, and its rules are checked. A brand book is read by sales, partners, and contractors, and compliance is judged by opinion.
- [RESEARCHER INFERENCE] The closest analog of a design system in branding is locked templates and asset libraries, not the document itself. This part of the experience can probably be transferred more boldly, the rest more cautiously.
- [RESEARCHER INFERENCE] Industry surveys ask the creators of systems: the same 2% at zeroheight shows the same weakness as marketers' surveys about brand books. The user's voice is almost absent.

### Gaps
- Rettig (1991): search returned a Practical Programmer column in CACM from 1991 with a different title, the page is closed. The author and the title Nobody reads documentation are not confirmed; it is an opinion column, not data. Do not use as evidence.
- Carroll, The Nurnberg Funnel: the book itself was not read, support only from the 1987 article.
- Sparkbox 2024 with the figure 28% is visible only in a secondary blog, not confirmed.

## 6. How rules work: the gap between what is written and practice, knowledge transfer

### Takeaway
[RESEARCHER INFERENCE] Useful as an explanatory frame, but not tested as applied to brand books. Studies of routines say that a gap between text and performance is normal. Szulanski shows that transfer is slowed mainly by knowledge barriers, not motivation. Jensen and Szulanski give quasi-experimental data in favor of a working example. This is one of the most practical transfers.

### Cited Findings
- [TRANSFER] Feldman, Pentland (2003), Administrative Science Quarterly 48(1): 94–118. A distinction between the ostensive aspect (the shared idea of a routine, possibly written down as a procedure) and the performative (specific actions of specific people). They are recursively linked, and so routines are a source of both stability and change. What transfers: a brand book is not equal to practice, and practice changes the understanding of the rule. Uncertainty: a theoretical work on routines, not on aesthetic norms. Access: SNIPPET. — [Sage](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.2307/3556620)
- [TRANSFER] Szulanski (1996), Strategic Management Journal 17 (Winter Special Issue): 27–43. 271 questionnaires, 122 transfers of 38 practices in eight companies, response rate 61%. Canonical R=0.87. Weights: lack of absorptive capacity in the recipient 0.53, causal ambiguity 0.34, an arduous relationship 0.33, recipient motivation 0.18, source motivation 0.05. The author warns that the sample may be too small for the stability of all weights except the three largest, but the general conclusion that knowledge barriers prevail over motivation holds. The survey is retrospective, correlational. What transfers: the problem is often not that people do not care, but that they cannot apply it and do not understand why it is so. Uncertainty: transfer of operational practices between units, not rule compliance. Access: FT-part. — [PDF](https://josephmahoney.web.illinois.edu/BADM%20545_Spring%202008/Paper/Szulanski%20(1996).pdf)
- [TRANSFER] Jensen, Szulanski (2007), Management Science 53(11): 1716–1730. Rank Xerox / Xerox Europe, eight years, three waves of transfers, 15 Western European countries, a natural quasi-experiment with repeated exposure. Using a template raises the effectiveness of transfer. According to Szulanski's comment, the second wave failed at equal top management support mainly because of the lack of a working example. The template here is a working example, not a written specification. The INSEAD article gives no comparison with documentation. Stronger in design than the rest of the section, but one case. Access: ABS and PAGE. — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v53y2007i11p1716-1730.html), [INSEAD](https://knowledge.insead.edu/strategy/knowledge-transfer-use-templates)
- [TRANSFER] The same source: advice to copy close to the example and adapt later, because preliminary changes get in the way of understanding the cause of failure. It contradicts the advice "let everyone adapt right away" and partly the frame of local systems. Access: PAGE. — [INSEAD](https://knowledge.insead.edu/strategy/knowledge-transfer-use-templates)
- [TRANSFER] Adler, Borys (1996): the distinction between enabling and coercive formalization (see section 3). Suitable for describing a brand book as a tool or as a police rulebook. There is no direct data on brand books. — [PDF](https://faculty.marshall.usc.edu/Paul-Adler/research/ASQ%20copy-1.pdf)

### Inferences
- [RESEARCHER INFERENCE] To explain shelfware this is useful in the following form: a brand book as a written-down routine inevitably diverges from performance; failure is more often tied to knowledge and relationships (did not understand why, cannot apply it, do not want to approach the brand team) than to unwillingness; working examples convey more than descriptions.
- [RESEARCHER INFERENCE] It agrees with the direct data: in van den Bosch 2006, socialization, knowledge of strategy, and tools resemble the relationships, causal ambiguity, and absorptive capacity in Szulanski. I see the similarity myself; no one has tested it.
- [RESEARCHER INFERENCE] The practical consequence for metrics: 100% compliance is the wrong goal if variation is normal. The goal must be phrased as telling acceptable variation from unacceptable. This is reasoning, not a result.

### Gaps
- Use only after checking, because the content was not read. Pentland, Feldman (2008) on designing routines: the [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.infoandorg.2008.08.001) leads to Elsevier, the text is unavailable. Hale, Borys (2013), Safety Science: [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ssci.2012.05.011). Brown, Duguid (1991), Organization Science: [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2.1.40). D'Adderio (2014), Organization Science: [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2014.0913). I added these four from memory as candidates; the DOIs resolve, but the claimed content is not confirmed.
- Szulanski, Jensen (2006), Strategic Management Journal 27(10): 937–957: only the reference in a bibliography is confirmed.
- I found no applications of routine theory to brand books.

## 8. Which failure patterns recur, and which of them are backed by data?

### Takeaway
[RESEARCHER INFERENCE] No pattern is confirmed by a direct causal study on brand books. Best supported: weak modeling by leaders, a bet on formal instruments only, problems of content and understanding, lack of working examples. The popular version "a brand book is forgotten because it is a boring PDF" has no evidence and meets directly opposite data.

| Leaders do not set an example | van den Bosch 2004; meta-analyses; Morhart 2009 | Moderate, mostly correlation, not about a document |
| Only formal instruments, no socialization | Henkel 2007; van den Bosch 2006 (EJM) | Moderate, self-report; there is a contradiction with vendor enforcement data |

- [RESEARCHER INFERENCE] A brand book quality checklist is probably not enough. The best data found point to organizational conditions that the document itself does not create. A skill that creates only a document solves only part of the task.
- [RESEARCHER INFERENCE] The most useful study that does not exist and that a company could run: portal logs, observation of tasks, an audit of released materials and comparison with claimed compliance. The 6% versus 3% gap in Lethbridge shows why a survey is not enough.
